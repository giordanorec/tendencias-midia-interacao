## 1. Resumo

AI left the screen, but what actually changed in 2026 was not the body — it was the place where the body learns. On 31 May 2026 NVIDIA released Cosmos 3, an open model that generates video, ambient sound *and action trajectories* in a single system; in February 2026 Waymo put DeepMind's Genie 3 underneath its driving simulator, with synchronised camera and lidar output. The simulator stopped being built and started being generated. At the same time, embodied competence remains rare and measurably poor: on the RoboDojo benchmark the best published generalist policy, π0.5, succeeds at **12.8%** of the 18 real-world tasks that a human teleoperator completes at **100%**. And generated worlds, however convincing, get physics wrong: on PhyGround no model exceeds 3.3/5, and rigid-body mechanics is the weakest domain. This map starts there — with a technology whose video is excellent and whose mechanics are not — rather than with the humanoid promise. The three root disruptions are the world becoming a model, the policy that transfers across worlds without seeing the code, and the body that became a commodity before the competence did. The consequence that matters most for people who design media and interaction is not the robot: it is that *designing a world has become designing a training curriculum*, and that whoever chooses the distribution of scenarios to generate is determining the behaviour of a machine that will act in the physical world.

## 2. O tema

The theme is the embodied agent — robot, drone, vehicle — and, above all, **the environment in which it learns**. Historically that environment was a simulator: a world written by hand by engineers, with authored geometry and contact parameters tuned by ear. The shift of 2025–2026 is that the environment is now *generated by a model* — the world model — trained on video and able to produce navigable scenes from a natural-language prompt.

This belongs to media and interaction through three doors, and none of them is robotics.

First: **the object of design changed**. Whoever designs a synthetic world is no longer designing a set for a human to look at; they are designing the distribution of experience from which an agent will form its behaviour. It is curriculum design wearing the clothes of level design.

Second: **the same artefact serves both sides**. The model that generates the training world for an autonomous car is the same kind of model that generates a playable world. Genie 3 is used by Waymo to simulate tornadoes and elephants on the road, and used by DeepMind to host the SIMA agent. The technical boundary between industrial simulator and interactive entertainment is dissolving — and that boundary was professional, not merely technical.

Third: **the digital environment becomes a surface for action**. A generalist agent that operates any 3D world without access to its code does not distinguish a game from a factory's digital twin. Anything with pixels and affordances becomes operable.

It deserves a future map because the asymmetry is large and visible: the capacity to *generate* world has grown fast and measurably; the capacity to *act* in the real world has grown slowly and remains poor. A map that treats these as the same curve will get everything wrong.

## 3. Onde isso está hoje

**Generative world models left the lab.** DeepMind's Genie 3 generates interactive environments in real time at 720p and 24 fps, holding consistency for "a few minutes" of continuous interaction, and accepts *promptable world events* — changing weather, introducing objects and characters through text. DeepMind lists the limitations without hedging: it cannot sustain interaction beyond a few minutes, cannot accurately simulate interactions between multiple independent agents, has no perfect geographic accuracy, and does not reliably render legible text.

**Industry has already bet on them.** In February 2026 Waymo announced the Waymo World Model, built on Genie 3 and adapted for driving, with multi-sensor output covering camera *and* lidar, controllable through driving actions, scene layouts and language prompts. The scenarios Waymo cites are precisely what cannot be collected on the road: tornadoes, floods, elephants, reckless drivers. It must be said that Waymo's post presents capability and **presents no validation methodology and no safety benchmarking**.

**NVIDIA opened the base.** On 31 May 2026, at GTC Taipei, Cosmos 3 was released: a mixture-of-transformers architecture pairing a reasoning transformer with an expert generation transformer, understanding object interactions, motion and spatio-temporal relationships *before* generating video and action trajectories. It generates text, image, video, ambient sound and action trajectories; it ships in Super, Nano and Edge variants and is available on Hugging Face and GitHub; the marketing claim is cutting training and evaluation cycles from months to days. The original platform paper (arXiv:2501.03575) states the thesis holding all of this up: *"Physical AI needs to be trained digitally first"* — it needs a digital twin of itself, the policy model, and a digital twin of the world, the world model.

**The generalist agent already transfers.** SIMA 2 is built on a Gemini foundation model; it converses, reasons about goals, operates a wide variety of 3D virtual worlds, "substantially closes the gap with human performance", generalises to previously unseen environments and — the strongest point — autonomously learns new skills from scratch in a new environment, using Gemini to generate the tasks and supply the reward. DeepMind has reported SIMA operating inside Genie 3-generated worlds with no knowledge of the underlying model.

**Embodied policies advanced and remain poor.** Physical Intelligence shipped π0 (31/10/2024), π0-FAST (04/02/2025), π0.5 (22/04/2025), π*0.6 (17/11/2025) and π0.7 (16/04/2026), the last described as "a step-change in generalization". And yet, on RoboDojo — a unified benchmark with 42 simulation tasks, 18 real-world tasks, five evaluation dimensions and 30 integrated policies — the finding is explicit: *"The best-performing policy, π0.5, achieves only a 12.8% overall success rate and a 22.9 score across 18 real-world tasks, while human teleoperation reaches a 100.0% success rate."*

**Generated worlds get physics wrong.** PhyGround evaluates adherence to physical law with 250 prompts, 13 laws, three domains (solid-body, fluid, optical), 459 annotators and 5,796 complete annotations. The result: no model exceeds **3.3/5** overall, and none crosses that threshold on solid-body mechanics either — the weakest domain, at a cross-model average of **2.79/5**. The pattern is consistent: visual quality rises, physical precision does not follow.

**Bodies got cheap and numerous.** Unitree reports roughly **18,000 cumulative bipedal humanoids as of July 2026**, of which more than 11,000 are the compact G1 — going to research labs, educational institutions and industrial clients. The same source reports no metric of work actually performed.

**The regulatory calendar closed in.** A German legal reading of embodied AI lists seven European regimes overlapping within roughly 24 months: AI Act transparency obligations on 02/08/2026; Cyber Resilience Act reporting on 11/09/2026; Data Act "data access by design" on 12/09/2026; Product Liability Directive transposition deadline on 09/12/2026; Machinery Regulation applicable on 20/01/2027; AI Act high-risk requirements between December 2027 and August 2028. The dedicated AI Liability Directive was **withdrawn in 2025** — what remains is strict product liability, with software and AI treated as products, the burden of proof eased for the injured party, and the liability chain extended to importers and distributors. The question the legal text itself leaves open is the question of this map: *what constitutes a "defect", and how far does the duty to monitor extend, if the product keeps evolving on its own after being placed on the market?*

**Maturity triage (Phase 2).** Explicit classification, using the method's own criterion: commoditised for 3+ years, or present in more than 50% of the target audience's estate ⇒ mature, and mature does not enter as a root.

## 4. As disrupções-raiz

### 4.1. O mundo deixa de ser construído e passa a ser gerado

**What it breaks.** It breaks the assumption that training an embodied agent requires someone to build the world first — model the geometry, author the materials, tune the contact parameters, script the scenario. It also breaks the economics of collection: the rare scenario no longer needs to happen in order to exist. And it breaks, sideways, the professional boundary between people who build industrial simulators and people who build playable worlds, because these become the same technical artefact.

**Why now.** Because three things happened in twelve months. Genie 3 stabilised real-time interaction at 720p/24fps with minutes of coherence and promptable events. Waymo took that to production, generating camera *and* lidar for long-tail scenarios the fleet has never seen. And Cosmos 3 opened the weights of a model that generates not only the world but the **action trajectory** inside it — closing the loop between environment and policy in a single system.

**What is still missing.** Verified physical fidelity, and institutional standing. On PhyGround no model exceeds 3.3/5, and solid-body mechanics — contact, which is precisely what a manipulator does — is the weakest domain at a 2.79/5 cross-model average. No standard yet says under what conditions a generated world *counts as evidence* that a system is safe. Without that, the generated world is a development tool and not a certification tool — and the difference is the difference between accelerating the project and replacing the test.

### 4.2. A política generalista: um corpo que transfere entre mundos sem ver o código

**What it breaks.** It breaks the one-robot-one-program industry: the automation integrator's margin lives in cell-specific programming, and a policy that accepts natural-language instruction plus demonstration removes that layer. It breaks the distinction between "NPC" and "robot controller" — SIMA 2 operates 3D worlds without access to the world's code, which is exactly the robot's condition before reality. And it breaks the premise that a digital environment is built only for humans to look at.

**Why now.** Because the π0 → π0.7 lineage showed, in 18 months, open-world generalisation growing with data scale rather than with task engineering, and because SIMA 2, backed by Gemini, demonstrated the missing piece: learning a new skill from scratch in a new environment by generating its own tasks and its own reward. That closes the self-improvement loop inside the generated world.

**What is still missing.** Data and evaluation. Teleoperation — the highest-fidelity data source — is capped at a few dozen episodes per operator-hour, with a human on the other end of every hour of the robot's day; it does not scale the way the web scaled for language. And evaluation is the larger hole: 12.8% against a human teleoperator's 100%, on RoboDojo, is the honest measure of the remaining distance. As long as training is cheaper than proving, the field will publish policies and not reliability.

### 4.3. O corpo virou commodity antes da competência

**What it breaks.** It inverts robotics' historical cost structure. It was: expensive, task-specific hardware amortised over years; a program written once for a fixed task. It becomes: cheap generic body, expensive generic competence, and neither tied to the other. That breaks the idea of a robot as capital equipment engineered for a function, and opens room for a market in which the body manufacturer, the skill vendor and the operator are three different companies.

**Why now.** Because Unitree accumulated roughly 18,000 bipeds by July 2026, more than 11,000 of them the compact G1, at an industrial and not an artisanal rate. The anthropomorphic body left the lab budget and entered the departmental budget.

**What is still missing.** The metric. Nobody publishes hours of autonomous useful work — the very source reporting the 18,000 units carries no operational data at all. As long as the unit of measure is "units produced" and not "hours of task completed without intervention", demonstration cannot be told apart from product. And the legal test is missing: the European stack — strict product liability from 09/12/2026, the Machinery Regulation on 20/01/2027 with explicit requirements on AI, self-learning functions and human-robot collaboration, AI Act high-risk through 2028 — has not yet met the concrete case of a machine that changed its behaviour after being sold.

**What the YAML cannot say.**

The wheel gives the impression of three parallel trees. They are not. **e2** (the gap between looking right and being right) is what holds **e8** (the missing metric) and **e9** (the regulatory collision) in the same place: all three are the same finding seen from different angles — *we do not know how to measure embodied competence*. If e2 were solved tomorrow, much of e8 and e9 would dissolve with it.

There is also a speed asymmetry that the YAML flattens by putting everything in years. The three roots run at different rates: the generated world runs at software speed (months), the body runs at manufacturing speed (quarters), and competence runs at the speed of human data collection — the slowest of the three, with no known shortcut. **Most wrong predictions about this theme come from applying the first speed to the other two.**

And one effect exists only at the intersection, which is why it fits in no single tree: if the world is generated (D1) *and* the agent transfers without seeing the code (D2), then a single piece of content — a world — is simultaneously a media product, a training environment and an attack surface. That crossing, not the humanoid, is what changes the work of people who design media and interaction.

## 6. Sinais fracos e wildcards

**Weak signal 1 — action became an output of the world model.** The most consequential detail of Cosmos 3 is not that it generates video, it is that it generates **action trajectories** in the same system that generates the environment. That means world and policy stop being two artefacts and become one. If it holds, the question "who made the simulator and who made the robot" stops having a separable answer — and the attribution of responsibility discussed in 4.3 loses its object.

**Weak signal 2 — the agent generates its own tasks.** SIMA 2 learns a new skill from scratch in a new environment by using Gemini to *generate the tasks and supply the reward*. This is small today and is the only known route around the human-data bottleneck. If it works on a physical body and not only in virtual worlds, RoboDojo's 12.8% ceiling rises in a way that no extrapolation of teleoperated collection predicts.

**Weak signal 3 — the body enters the reasoning.** Systems that treat physical constraint as part of the execution model rather than as a downstream check (the case of ROSClaw, cited in the class's own scan) invert a layer separation robotics has held for thirty years: plan first, check feasibility after. If the body enters the reasoning, planning stops being transferable across bodies — which contradicts the generalist policy promise head-on.

**Weak signal 4 — generated worlds with sound.** Models generating ambient audio alongside image (Cosmos 3 does; commercial world models announce the same) open a modality nobody is evaluating. There is no acoustic-plausibility benchmark analogous to PhyGround. This is where error will go unnoticed longest — and where people from media hold more accumulated competence than the robotics field does.

**Wildcard A — the accident whose cause is in the simulator.** Serious physical harm whose investigation concludes that the fault lay neither in the robot nor in the model, but in the **distribution of worlds it was trained on** — a scenario the generator never produced, or produced with wrong physics. Immediate consequence: synthetic-world forensics (e1.2.1) stops being hypothetical and becomes a requirement, and the cost of generating a world stops being the cost of the prompt and becomes the cost of defending it in court.

**Wildcard B — a regulator rules that generated worlds are not evidence.** If an authority decides that hours of simulation in a generative model do not count toward conformity assessment — being neither reproducible nor physically auditable — the entire economics of disruption 4.1 flips sign overnight, and the advantage returns to whoever owns a real fleet collecting real data.

**Wildcard C — the swarm arrives before the humanoid.** The drone swarm (the fire-rescue case, brought in almost by accident in the class's scan) is the form of physical AI with the lowest demand for fine manipulation — which is exactly where policies fail. It is plausible that the first physical-AI application with mass public impact has neither arms nor legs, and that the humanoid, which absorbs all the attention, arrives later.

**Wildcard D — an open world model good enough kills the proprietary simulator.** Cosmos 3's weights are public. If the community reaches, through fine-tuning, the missing contact fidelity, the licensed industrial simulation market empties out within one or two purchasing cycles.

## 7. Contra o próprio mapa

**The map may be picking the wrong side of the asymmetry.** All of section 3 is built on two numbers saying "generation is doing well, action is doing badly" — 3.3/5 on PhyGround, 12.8% on RoboDojo. These are September 2026 measurements and they are *benchmark* measurements, not production ones. Benchmarks tend to measure what is hard and ignore what already works: there may be a set of narrow industrial tasks where the real rate is far higher and nobody publishes it because it is academically uninteresting. If so, the map underestimates the speed of D2 and D3 and every date in the wheel is too late.

**A 2031 horizon is short for third-order effects.** Restructuring professional training (e1.1.1, e4.1.1, e3.1.1) historically takes more than five years between signal and curricular change. Placing those effects in 2030–2031 is structural optimism. It is more honest to read them as "began in 2031", not "completed".

**Source bias: nearly everything here comes from sellers.** NVIDIA, DeepMind, Waymo and Physical Intelligence are interested parties, and the documents I read are announcements. Waymo's post, as recorded in section 3, presents capability and no validation. The two counterweights I used — PhyGround and RoboDojo — are academic and independent, but they are *two*. The balance of evidence in this map tilts toward those with commercial interest in the result.

**Confirmation bias against humanoid hype.** I assumed, and said more than once, that the humanoid is overrated. That is a comfortable, sceptical position, and scepticism is a bias too. Disruption 4.3 was written to deflate enthusiasm, and may therefore have over-discounted manufacturing learning effects: 18,000 units produced teach things about mechanical reliability that 18 prototypes do not.

**The map is too agreeable to its audience.** It was commissioned for people who design media and interaction, and it concludes that designing worlds becomes a core robotics competence (e1.1, e3.1.1). That is exactly what this audience wants to hear. I flagged those effects for review in Phase 5 and kept them — which means this map carries a bias it identified and did not correct.

**Energy and materials are missing.** Nothing here covers GPU consumption for real-time world generation, nor the supply chain for actuators and rare-earth magnets — which is plausibly the real bottleneck of D3 and is entirely outside this map.

**5. ## 9. Três cenários para 2031

### 9.1. Provável

The generated world won as a development tool and lost as an instrument of proof. Between 2027 and 2029 nearly every serious robotics team moved to generating its training environments rather than building them, and the gain was real: scenario variety stopped being a budget line and became a choice. But no authority accepted generative simulation hours as evidence of conformity, and so physical testing never shrank — it merely stopped growing. The stack that consolidated was hybrid, exactly as engineers predicted and announcements did not: generated world for variety, rigid engine for contact, real collection for calibration.

Generalist policies improved considerably and stayed far from humans on long-horizon tasks. Real manipulation success rates, measured on the public benches that became common field infrastructure, moved from just over 10% to somewhere between 40% and 60%, depending on who was measuring — and the argument about *how* to measure consumed more conferences than the argument about how to train. The automation integrator did not disappear; it changed business, and those who survived sold evaluation and warranty rather than code.

Bodies got cheap and plentiful, and most of them sat still. A market for rented robot-hours existed and stayed small, because the friction was never access to a body — it was knowing whether the policy was reliable in that particular warehouse. In Europe, the practical answer to the regulatory collision was to freeze: a robot circulating in open space is a robot with its policy locked at sale; the robot that learns continuously stayed inside private facilities, under an operator. In Brazil, adoption came through the application layer, with imported hardware and imported policy, and the competence that formed locally was integration, collection and operation — the same position the country held in the previous wave.

And the change that most affected people who design media and interaction did not come from robotics. It came from discovering that the world you design is a curriculum: that the choice of which scenarios to generate, and in what proportion, determines a machine's behaviour — and that this choice had no formal owner in any organisation.

### 9.2. Desejável

The field learned to measure before it learned to sell. The pressure came from outside: between 2027 and 2028, per-law physics evaluation — what PhyGround started — became a purchasing requirement, and no world-model vendor could sell video quality as though it were physics quality any longer. Reports began carrying per-domain performance, with solid-body, fluid and optical reported separately, and it became publicly visible that contact was everyone's weak point.

Sim-and-real benches with standardised embodiments became a public good, funded by consortium, and the effect matched what open datasets had done for computer vision twenty years earlier: it stopped being possible to announce a policy without a comparable number. World auditing became routine practice — generator, seed and prompt versioned alongside the model — and when incidents occurred they were reproducible, investigable and fixable without anyone having to guess at the cause.

Human demonstration was recognised as labour and paid as such, with explicit licensing of captured gesture; unions arrived early to the discussion rather than late. And interaction design schools folded distribution specification into the curriculum, so that the person deciding what an agent will see during training knows what they are deciding and answers for it.

In Brazil, work data captured locally — gesture, factory floor, street — was treated as a national asset rather than as raw material for export: there was counterpart in model access and in installed capacity. It was little, and it was more than happened with language data.

### 9.3. Indesejável

Visual fidelity beat physical precision, and nobody noticed in time. Between 2027 and 2029 the generated world got good enough to convince executives and buyers, and the difference between looking right and being right stopped being discussed because no mandatory metric surfaced it. Teams cut physical testing on the strength of simulation hours nobody knew how to audit. The accident came — not one, several — and each investigation hit the same wall: the world that policy trained in had not been versioned, the generator had been updated midway, and nothing could be re-executed.

The regulatory response was late and blunt, as responses to accidents tend to be. It restricted by machine category rather than by quality of evidence, which punished those who documented and failed to reach those who did not. The fragmented chain — one builds the body, another sells the policy, a third operates it — worked as a responsibility diffuser: each link pointed to the next, and strict product liability, designed for products that do not change, could not say at what moment the defect arose in a thing that learns on its own.

Demonstration work settled into precarity: paying someone badly to teach a machine to do what that person does became a market segment, without contract, licence or counterpart. And Brazil entered as it always enters — buying bodies, renting policies, exporting data for free — with the difference that this time the exported data was the record of how Brazilians work.

Finally, and more quietly: digital environments designed for people came to be operated by agents without anyone having designed for that, and the response was defensive and ugly — interfaces deliberately degraded to confuse machines, which confused people too.

## 10. O experimento

**World proof: does whoever gets the appearance right get the mechanics right?**

This map's central claim is that perceptual fidelity and physical precision have diverged. That is testable by one student, in one week, with a phone and a laptop.

**Setup.** Choose **three laws of solid-body mechanics** — PhyGround's weakest domain — that can be staged on a tabletop: (a) elastic collision of two spheres, (b) friction and arrest of a block on a ramp at varying incline, (c) toppling through displacement of the centre of mass. For each law, **ten variations** of initial condition, with the varied parameter recorded (mass, angle, height).

**Three productions of the same scene, thirty times over.**
1. **Real**: film with a phone at 60 fps, ruler in frame, fixed angle.
2. **Rigid engine**: reproduce in MuJoCo with the same measured parameters.
3. **Generated world**: generate from a prompt in a public-weights world model (Cosmos 3 is the natural choice, being open and generating video; any conditioned video generator serves the test).

**Two measurements, and the contrast between them is the point.**

*Objective, per law.* Extract from the image what the law predicts — object centre position frame by frame, critical slip angle, velocity ratio after collision — and compare each production against the real one. A 1-to-5 rubric per law, PhyGround-style, and not a single aggregate score: the whole point is that the aggregate hides the hole in contact.

*Perceptual, with people.* Show video pairs — real and generated — to **ten people**, without saying which is which, and ask two things: which looks real, and what happened physically (did the block stop or slide? which sphere left faster?).

**What the experiment decides.** If people identify the real one but get the physics wrong in both, the problem is human. If people *cannot* tell real from generated **and** the objective measure shows large mechanical error, this map's thesis is empirically supported on a kitchen table: the generated world passes the eye test and fails the law test — and training a body in it means training in a world that fools the evaluator. If the generated one matches physics within MuJoCo's own margin, disruption 4.1 is more mature than this document assumes, and every date in the wheel is too late.

**Cost and time.** A phone, a table, two blocks, two marbles, MuJoCo (free), credits or a GPU for the generator. One person-week. **Deliverable**: a table of 3 laws × 10 variations × 3 productions, plus the perceptual accuracy matrix across the ten evaluators.

**Extension, if time allows.** Repeat law (c), toppling, with audio on, and ask the same people whether the impact *sound* is plausible. There is no public benchmark of acoustic plausibility in world models — that is weak signal 4 from section 6, and it is where people from media hold an advantage over people from robotics.
